The Trainer Playbook

Content selection, run sheets, methods, FAQ background, and checklists. Read once, then return by section.

← Trainer hub

Your ready-to-use kit for delivering the “AI in Sales” program to sales colleagues in your region. This playbook does not re-teach the AI content — the learning platform carries that. It shows you how to read a group, pick the right content from the platform, run a session with it, answer the hard questions, and roll out with confidence.

1. How to Use This Playbook

Who this is for

You are an international Kärcher trainer who will deliver the AI in Sales program to sales colleagues in your country. You do not need to be an AI expert. You need to be a confident facilitator who can explain a clear structure, run a few hands-on moments, and handle questions honestly. This playbook assumes you have attended the Train-the-Trainer workshop, or will.

The participants you will train are mostly Basic or Intermediate with AI (91% in our survey). That is good news: you are not competing with experts. You are showing busy salespeople a few concrete moves that save them time this week.

What you deliver from

Everything lives on the learning platform: karcher-ai-sales.web.app, sign-in with the Kärcher Google account. You do not rebuild content; you open it.

The platform has five doors:

  • Quick help — one task card per everyday job, sorted in groups from emails to coaching, each one a copied prompt and about five minutes, with ready-made practice material on the card. The newest group, Sales Coaching & Practice, holds practice Gems from the trainer community.
  • AI Basics — short foundations (what AI is for, prompting, Gemini & Gems, Deep Research, NotebookLM, Workspace Studio, data safety).
  • The sales-cycle course — the nine steps from acquisition to follow-up, worked end to end on one example customer (NorthStar), each step with an idea page, a quick check, and recipes: the exact prompt, what to attach, the Gem one click away, and a ready-made example Doc to compare with.
  • The guide — the full handbook behind everything, including the sales-leadership track.
  • The notebook — the same handbook as a knowledge base: ask in your own words, get answers with citations.

Every lesson can also be listened to, and a guided tour introduces the platform by itself — click “Start the tour” on the start page.

On top of the platform you have six things of your own:

  • The pre-survey (Google Forms) — your selection instrument: make your own copy of the form, then send your copy’s link, so the answers land in your own response Sheet. Section 3 explains how to read them.
  • The Train-the-Trainer catalog deck — the slide catalog you received at the workshop: every door, card, lesson screen, and example output as a slide. Use it as your flip-book and projector fallback; in the room, prefer showing the platform itself.
  • The session decks — ready-to-present decks for participants, one per session format, each with the complete spoken script in the notes and “Your turn” exercise slides that carry the click paths: the Basics session (the tools, prompting, the data gate), the team-meeting intro (fifteen minutes, everyone runs one card), and the course sprint (the nine steps, every document built by the group). Present them as they are, hide what your session does not need, or translate them for your market.
  • The Session-Planner Gem — attach the response Sheet of your pre-survey copy, tell it the session size, and it drafts your plan with the program’s reading rules: blocks, material, opening quotes, what to skip. Open the Session-Planner Gem.
  • The Trainer Notebook (NotebookLM) — this playbook, the catalog deck, and the pre-survey as a knowledge base. Tell it your time budget, group, and focus topics, and it drafts a session plan you shape; it answers only from these materials, with citations. Open the Trainer Notebook (public link, no sign-in needed).
  • This playbook.

The one habit that carries everything

Select per group, then deliver. Do not walk every group through everything. Send the pre-survey first, read which cluster the group falls into and what they need most, pick the matching doors and lessons, then deliver with NorthStar as the one running example. Everything else in this playbook supports that single habit.

2. Program at a Glance

One continuous sales-cycle flow

The program is not a list of AI tricks. Its core, the course behind the third door, follows the Kärcher sales cycle as one continuous flow, from acquisition all the way to follow-up. The sales cycle itself is the structure, and each step’s output becomes the next step’s input.

    B --> C["3 · First Contact"]
    C --> D["4 · Conversation Preparation"]
    D --> E["5 · Needs Analysis"]
    E --> F["6 · Value Argumentation"]
    F --> G["7 · Presentation & Demonstration"]

One fictional customer, NorthStar Facility Services Benelux, travels through every step — with its own website, a named buying center (Lars Meijer, Sophie Verbeek, Milan Bakker, Eva van Dijk), and a real product on the table (a pilot of three BR 45/22 scrubber driers, specs from the internal YellowFile). Because NorthStar is invented and cannot be researched, live research is demonstrated on ISS (issworld.com), a real, public facility-services company that stands in for a NorthStar-type account.

Session sizes

Your live sessions realistically come in three sizes — plan in these, each backed by a ready session deck:

Size What fits inside Deck
One hour The Basics session as it is: tools, prompting, data gate, one card hands-on Basics session
Ninety minutes Basics, then a hands-on block: the cards the group’s answers put on top Basics session + cards
Two hours Basics compact, then two or three sales-cycle steps on NorthStar Basics session + Sprint (steps hidden)

Two smaller shapes exist around these: the team-meeting intro (15 minutes in an existing meeting, one card — creates demand; own deck ready) and card of the week (no session: one Quick-Help card per week, compared in the Monday call). A half or full day runs the whole cycle for a group that asks for it (the Sprint deck in full). Times are a budget, not a script.

Required accounts

Google Workspace with Gemini (Sheets, Docs, Slides, Gmail), Deep Research, Gems, and NotebookLM, signed in with the Kärcher account — the same sign-in unlocks the platform. Confirm participants can sign in before the session. For data exercises, the practice tracker is linked right on the pipeline card, so nobody needs to bring real data.

3. Select the Right Content per Group

This is the part that makes the program fit, instead of being one fixed agenda for everyone. A single training cannot be optimal for a Key Account Manager and a Sales Director at the same time, so you assemble the right one in minutes.

Step 1 — Send the pre-survey

First, make your own copy of the pre-survey — one click, and the copy is yours. Send that copy’s link to participants a few days before the session; it is the same form you answered at the Train-the-Trainer workshop. Copying matters: only the owner of a form sees its responses, so your own copy is what puts your group’s answers into your hands (in Forms, the Responses tab creates the Sheet you attach to the Session-Planner Gem). It takes participants about five minutes and asks:

  1. Focus — the cluster question: “Do you mainly sell yourself (own accounts and deals) or mainly steer a team (pipeline, coaching, strategy)?” This single question separates Cluster A from Cluster B and resolves edge cases (an Online Director, for example, lands in B).
  2. AI experience — Beginner / Basic / Intermediate / Advanced, plus tools used.
  3. Time drains — which sales activities cost the most time (up to 3).
  4. What the session should cover — a usefulness grid whose rows are worded like the platform on purpose: rows 1–6 are the six Quick-Help groups, row 7 is the course.
  5. Expectations and concerns — free text; the concerns steer your opening.

Aim for responses from at least half the group; a clear pattern usually appears quickly. Read everything in the Forms summary view.

Step 2 — Read the result

You are looking for three things:

  • Which cluster dominates (job to be done, not channel):
    • Cluster A — Key Account & Field Sales: KAMs, direct reps, business development. Complex consultative selling, several stakeholders, individual offers.
    • Cluster B — Sales Leadership & Management: directors, area/national sales managers, center managers. Steering pipeline, coaching, strategy, reporting.
  • How much foundations time to plan. A Beginner/Basic majority means: open with the guided tour and one or two AI Basics (prompting and data safety first) before anything else.
  • Which content tops the grid. The top rows literally name what to open. From the original survey across 75 colleagues, the pattern is reliable: data & pipeline is the number-one need, then meeting prep & research, then proposals & content. If your group mirrors this, you already know where to spend time.

Step 3 — Pick the content

Four patterns cover most groups:

Signal in the results What you open
Beginners dominate The guided tour, then Basics: prompting + data safety
Data & pipeline on top The Your-data cards; See your pipeline at a glance first
Sellers (Cluster A) The course: steps 1–9, full depth on research, needs analysis, value argument
Leaders (Cluster B) Your-data cards, step 9 (follow-up & steering), the leadership chapters in the guide, and the routine moves (card of the week)

Then pour it into one of the three session formats (Section 2). Concerns from the survey get addressed openly at the start — the data-safety Basic answers most of them.

Worked example: two groups, two agendas

Group A — twelve Key Account Managers, mostly Intermediate, top needs research and proposals. Their day-to-day is multi-stakeholder consultative selling, so they get a course sprint:

Tour (2 min) → data-safety Basic → the course: step 2 Research (heavy, with the live ISS demo) → step 3 First contact → step 5 Needs analysis → step 6 Value argument → step 7 Presentation & demo → step 9 Follow-up → commitments. ~4 hours. Accent: full lesson depth (idea → recipe → example Doc) on steps 2, 5, and 6; the translation card as a side bite.

Group B — ten Area Sales Managers and Center Managers, top needs pipeline and reporting. They steer teams rather than run individual deals, so the agenda is shorter and tilts to oversight and enablement:

Tour (2 min) → See your pipeline at a glance (everyone runs it on the practice tracker) → Turn your sheet into a short report → step 9 Follow-up & steering → the leadership chapters in the guide (pipeline steering, coaching) → how to run “card of the week” with their own teams → commitments. ~3 hours. Accent: leaders leave with the routine they will install, not just their own skills.

Same platform, same NorthStar example, two clearly different days. The pre-survey did the sorting; you made the call.

4. Run Sheet / Regiebuch

This is your facilitation guide per unit: the goal, the lead Gem, the key point, and the one thing not to skip. It does not reproduce the content — the lesson on the platform IS the content, with the exact prompt, the attachment, and the example Doc on screen. Keep the platform open; present from it.

How the chain works: every step hands its output to the next, all on NorthStar. Acquisition fills the funnel → research produces a briefing and a Go/No-Go → the buying-center map targets the first email → discovery produces a need profile → value produces an argument → presentation and demo turn it into a decision moment → follow-up closes the loop. Always name the hand-off out loud (“the briefing you just made is what you attach in the next step”). That continuity is what makes the day feel like one workflow, not a string of demos.

The value-hypothesis thread runs underneath it all. Name it when it appears: the first value hypothesis (why Kärcher might matter to this customer) forms in research (step 2), feeds the first email (step 3) and the discovery questions (step 5), is confirmed against the real need in discovery, and becomes the substantiated value argument (step 6). Pointing to that thread is what keeps each output specific to NorthStar instead of generic.

Opening block — the tour + the gate (~20 min)

  • Goal: the platform introduces itself; the data green light and the two-step gate are installed before any tool use.
  • How: click “Show me around” (the tour speaks for itself), then open the data-safety Basic together.
  • Key points: in approved Workspace tools, signed in, real customer data is allowed — share only what the task needs, then verify; the gate is two steps, Prompt with the right context, Validate the output; formal proposals, pricing, and contract terms keep the normal sign-off.
  • The one thing not to skip: the green light paired with the human-in-the-loop rule. If you skip this, the data-security worry follows you all day.

Step 1 — Acquisition (~20 min, A)

  • Goal: two roads to a lead — inbound (industry briefing → posts, be the visible expert) and outbound (a sourced target shortlist).
  • Lead Gems: Industry-Research Gem, Post-Gem, Lead-Finder Gem.
  • Key point: the briefing is internal, never published; each post shares one insight and ends in a question, never a pitch.
  • Don’t skip: the hand-off — a reply to a post, or a shortlist name, is the lead that step 2 researches.

Step 2 — Research & Qualification (~30 min, the live moment)

  • Goal: a company name becomes a sourced briefing and a clear decision: pursue or pass.
  • Lead Gem: Account-Briefing Gem with Deep Research — run it live on ISS; it is the most persuasive moment of the day (pre-run a fallback).
  • Key point: facts separated from hypotheses; the rep makes the Go/No-Go, never the AI. The value-hypothesis thread starts here.
  • Don’t skip: verifying one claim against its source in front of the room.

Step 3 — First Contact (~20 min, A)

  • Goal: map the buying center from the briefing, then one role-fit first email that wins a meeting, not the deal.
  • Lead Gems: Buying-Center Gem, then the first-email recipe.
  • Key point: the four NorthStar personas and the role logic (decision-maker → impact, procurement → cost and risk, user → daily work); the email stays a Gmail draft until the rep sends it. A translation pass is the international accent.
  • Don’t skip: the map returns in steps 4 and 5 — say so.

Step 4 — Conversation Preparation (~10 min, A, light)

  • Goal: prepare the human side — opener, what to listen for, a loose style read.
  • Lead Gem: Rapport Gem (map + the person’s actual reply go in).
  • Key point: the style read is a hypothesis to hold lightly, read live in the room, never an AI profile from public data. Prepare with AI, then put the tool down.
  • Don’t skip: the one line — the meeting itself stays human.

Step 5 — Needs Analysis (~25 min, the middle of the cycle)

  • Goal: sharp questions in, a clear need profile out.
  • Lead Gem: Needs-Analysis Gem, two recipes: the question set before the meeting, tagging the notes after.
  • Key point: questions get the customer talking about their situation and needs — not pitching; the profile tags six dimensions with evidence, and a gap becomes a follow-up question, never a guess.
  • Don’t skip: the link that discovery must precede argumentation.

Step 6 — Value Argumentation (~25 min, core)

  • Goal: the confirmed need profile becomes a customer-specific value argument.
  • Lead Gem: Value-Argument Gem (need profile + BR 45/22 YellowFile attached).
  • Key point: feature → advantage → benefit → check, ordered by confirmed needs, in two role variants (Lars operational, Sophie commercial/total-cost); every product fact from the YellowFile, prices never in customer-facing output.
  • Don’t skip: every benefit must trace back to a discovered need — cut what does not map.

Step 7 — Presentation & Demonstration (~20 min, core)

  • Goal: a lean three-slide story (situation, fit, decision) and a demo storyboard that tell one story.
  • Lead Gems: the presentation recipe (Canvas → Slides draft, content only) and the Demo Gem.
  • Key point: the rep applies the Kärcher template and CI before anything goes out; every slide claim has a matching proof in the demo.
  • Don’t skip: the quality check before “sending” — substance, data, fit, next step.

Step 8 — Closing (~15 min, calm by design)

  • Goal: read the buying signal, choose the right path, answer objections calmly, capture the result.
  • Lead Gem: the closing coach (value block + short meeting notes in).
  • Key point: in the worked example the signal is weak and the coach recommends clarifying value first — an honest recommendation, not a pushy one; the price objection gets a three-move answer (understand, reframe to cost-over-time, concrete next step).
  • Don’t skip: the summary keeps agreed and open points separate, with no invented commitments.

Step 9 — Follow-up & Steering (~20 min)

  • Goal: the meeting summary becomes a follow-up email, a four-line handover, one owned next step, and a light account segment.
  • Lead Gem: Follow-up & Handover Gem.
  • Key point: sent within twenty-four hours; where no date was agreed, the output says “to verify” instead of inventing one — the AI marks the gap, the rep closes it.
  • Don’t skip: one concrete next step with a named owner — it is what makes the loop real.

Leadership block (Cluster B, ~30 min)

  • Goal: leaders steer with the same tools — pipeline reading, coaching cues, team routine.
  • Material: the Your-data cards (pipeline at a glance, sheet to report) run on the practice tracker; the leadership chapters in the guide (pipeline steering, forecast, coaching); the Pipeline-Steering and Coaching & Steering Gems.
  • Key point: AI sorts the list, the leader decides which account deserves the team’s week; the leader’s strongest move is installing the routine — the link in the team meeting, the card of the week, one question in the meeting after.
  • Don’t skip: leaders leave with the routine they will install, not just their own skills.

5. Methods Guide

Teaching AI to a mixed-experience sales room

Most of your room is Basic or Intermediate. Three principles keep them with you:

  • Lead with the pain, not the technology. Open each unit with the work problem (“a busy week that moved nothing”), then show AI as the relief. Never open with “let me explain how large language models work.”
  • One concrete win per unit. Aim for each person to leave each block with something they could use Monday: a ranked list, a briefing, a three-slide outline. Concrete beats comprehensive.
  • Show, then hand over. Demonstrate the move on NorthStar, then immediately have them repeat it — the lessons are built for exactly this, every recipe has the prompt to copy and the example Doc to compare with. The “now do it with your own account” handoff (Section 6) is where learning sticks.

Keep frameworks in the background. The need dimensions and value chains are useful logic underneath the lessons, but they are tools, not subjects. Say “better discovery questions,” not an acronym. And let the platform do its share of the talking: the tour introduces the structure, the Listen buttons read lessons aloud, the quizzes lock ideas in — you facilitate, you do not lecture.

Demoing tools live without losing the room

Live demos are powerful and risky. Protect yourself:

  • Have a fallback. Keep a screenshot or a pre-run result of each demo (especially the ISS Deep Research run, which can take minutes). The Train-the-Trainer catalog deck carries every screen and real output as a slide — it is your ready-made fallback. If the live run stalls, show the slide and narrate.
  • Narrate the prompt, not the typing. Read the recipe’s prompt aloud as you copy it so people learn the thinking, not the keystrokes.
  • Pre-run the slow ones. Deep Research takes time. Start the live ISS run, then talk through the buying center while it works, and return to the result.
  • Use the side panel where it lives. For presentations, work in Google Slides with Gemini in the side panel so people see the real workflow, not a separate chat window.
  • Keep accounts ready. Confirm sign-ins before you start. Nothing kills momentum like ten people stuck at a login screen.

Making the gate a habit, not a lecture

  • The quality gate (Prompt · Validate): do not deliver it as a compliance lecture. Make it a reflex you model. Every time you prompt, narrate the green light out loud (“NorthStar is fictional, so I prompt freely; with a real account in an approved tool I would still share only the fields the task needs”). Every time AI returns numbers, pause and ask the room “would you send this to a customer as-is?” That two-second pause, repeated, is the lesson.
  • The green light, always visible: in approved tools, signed in, real customer data is allowed; the rep’s job is to share the fields the task needs and verify the output. In sessions we model this on NorthStar (fictional, so prompt freely) and ISS (real and public, so use it for live research and verify against the source).
  • The check lives in the recipes. Every lesson recipe ends with its own check line (ground every number, no invented commitments, trace benefits to needs). Point at it; you never have to carry the rules alone.

Managing energy, timing, and group dynamics

  • Budget, don’t script. The unit times are a budget. If step 2 runs long because the ISS demo lands well, take it from a lighter unit, not from the opening gate.
  • Adapt depth to the room:
    • Skeptical group — go slower on the gate and verification, lean on the worked example and the real result Docs, invite their worries early (Section 7), and let one good result speak for itself. Do not oversell.
    • Enthusiastic group — move faster through demos, spend the saved time on “now do it with your own account,” and stretch into more cards.
  • Protect the hands-on time. When you are behind, cut your own talking, not the participants’ practice. The doing is the value.
  • Read the energy after lunch. A natural dip: reset it with a Quick-Help card everyone runs — a result in five minutes beats any energizer.

6. Exercise Instructions

Each interactive element follows the same rhythm: you demo on NorthStar → they do it on NorthStar → they redo it on their own account. The prompts live on the lesson screens (copy button); the ready-made NorthStar Docs are linked right on each recipe, so nobody types context by hand.

The five-minute pipeline win (the strongest opener)

  1. Everyone opens Quick help → See your pipeline at a glance.
  2. They copy the practice tracker (one click on the card) — no real data needed.
  3. They copy the prompt, run it in Sheets, open the new tab.
  4. Compare totals across the room; verify one total by hand.
  • Good output: the pipeline-value pivot by stage and segment, on a new tab.
  • Feedback: the verification step is the lesson — “trust it, then check one number.”

The prompt rewrite drill (Basics block)

  1. Show a weak one-line prompt (e.g. “write me an email to a customer”).
  2. Rebuild it live along the prompting Basic’s structure — say what you want, give the context, name the format, ask it to flag what it does not know.
  3. Pairs rewrite a second weak prompt for a NorthStar task.
  4. Two or three pairs read theirs out; the room decides Go / Revise / Stop on one AI output.
  • Good output: a prompt where the context and the wanted format are explicit and the check line asks AI to flag unsupported claims.
  • Feedback: praise specificity; push back gently on vague context (“what does the model actually need to know here?”).

Live ISS Deep Research demo + NorthStar briefing (step 2)

  1. You launch the Account-Briefing Gem on ISS live (pre-run fallback ready).
  2. While it runs, walk the room through the NorthStar buying center: Lars (ops), Sophie (procurement), Milan (site), Eva (HSE).
  3. When the result returns, verify one claim against its source in front of everyone.
  4. Participants read the ready-made ISS briefing Doc from the lesson and make the Go/No-Go call themselves.
  • Good output: a defended Go or No-Go, with the two or three to-verify claims named.
  • Feedback: flag any claim presented as fact that was actually a hypothesis — this is where verification matters most.

Discovery questions and the need profile (step 5)

  1. Participants run recipe 1 with the ready-made briefing and map attached: the question set.
  2. They compare it with the example Doc on the lesson.
  3. They run recipe 2 on the ready-made visit notes: the tagged need profile.
  4. They name the gaps and write one follow-up question per gap.
  • Good output: a question set that gets the customer talking (not pitching), and a profile where strong needs carry evidence and gaps are named, not guessed.
  • Feedback: check that questions are open and high-gain, not product pitches in disguise.

The value block and the three-slide pitch (steps 6–7)

  1. From the need profile and the YellowFile, participants run the value-argument recipe.
  2. They compare with the real example output (the NorthStar Value Block with the Lars and Sophie variants).
  3. They run the presentation recipe: three slides, situation → fit → decision, one idea per slide.
  4. They reframe for one stakeholder and run the quality check before “sending.”
  • Good output: every benefit traceable to a confirmed need; three slides, one message each; the role variant actually changes the argument, not just the wording.
  • Feedback: the most common miss is a fourth slide or a brochure tone. Push them to cut anything that does not move the decision.

The “now do it with your own account” handoff

This is the close of every exercise, and the real payoff. After the NorthStar round, say: “NorthStar was the worked example. Now open one of your own live accounts and run the same recipe — in our approved tools, signed in, that is allowed; share what the task needs and verify the output.” Give them two or three minutes. This converts a demo into a habit they own. Make time for it even when you are behind — it is the single most valuable minute in each block.

7. FAQ & Objection Handling

These are the real worries from the survey (n=75), in roughly the order they came up. The two biggest are data security/compliance and trust in AI numbers (hallucinations). Closing/objection skill was the lowest need, so do not over-prepare for sales-technique pushback — prepare for these adoption worries instead. Every answer anchors in the gate and the human-in-the-loop rule.

“Is this safe? What about data security and compliance?”

This is the number-one worry, so meet it head-on, early, with the gate — not as a disclaimer at the end.

Reframe: “Good news first: in our approved tools, signed in with your Kärcher account, you are allowed to work with real customer data — the Workspace contract keeps it confidential and the tools never train on it. Your part is light but real: share only the information the task actually needs, then verify the output. To rank a pipeline, AI needs stage, value, and dates, not a customer’s phone number. Less data is both safer and sharper. In the sessions we practise on NorthStar, which is fictional, so you can prompt freely, and on ISS, which is real and public, so you use it for live research and verify it.”

Point to the Prompt · Validate gate from the data-safety Basic, and note that the normal sign-off still applies to formal proposals, pricing, and contract terms.

“Won’t AI just replace me?”

Reframe: “AI does the sorting, drafting, and structuring. It does not decide which account deserves your week, build the relationship in the room, or own the customer promise. The line is exact: AI sorts the list, you decide which account deserves your week. The reps who win are the ones who let AI take the busywork so they spend more time with customers, not less.”

“How can I trust the numbers? It makes things up.”

This is the second-biggest worry, and a legitimate one.

Reframe: “You are right not to trust it blindly — that is the job, not a flaw in the training. Confident output is not verified output. So we build two reflexes: tell AI to flag missing data rather than invent it, and run the Validate step before anything customer-facing. The platform’s own outputs model this: in the real NorthStar follow-up, where no date was agreed, the document says ‘to verify’ instead of inventing one. AI drafts; you verify; and the normal sign-off still applies to anything formal that goes out.”

“I’ll lose my own voice and style.”

Reframe: “AI gives you a fast first draft, not a final send. Your voice comes from the editing pass — the part you keep. The first-contact lesson is explicit: the email stays a Gmail draft until you have read it, edited it, and send it in your own words. A draft you reshape is still your voice; a blank page at 11pm is the real threat to it.”

“The output isn’t in Kärcher corporate identity / our brand.”

Reframe: “Correct, and we don’t expect raw output to be customer-ready. AI gets you to a strong draft fast; you then apply the Kärcher template, the approved claims, and the CI before anything goes out. The presentation lesson builds this in: the draft exports as content only, the final deck is built in our templates — and for customer-facing decks the normal sign-off still applies on top.”

“Is the effort worth the benefit? I’m already busy.”

Reframe: “That busy week that moved nothing is exactly the problem this solves. The first wins are small and immediate: your pipeline value in one prompt, a customer deck outline in minutes instead of an evening. Start with one card — the card of the week is exactly that, one use case, one habit. You don’t adopt everything; you adopt one thing that gives time back this week.”

“We already had an AI training from IT.”

Reframe: “Good — this is the other half. IT teaches how the tools work. This program teaches where they fit in a sales week: the platform’s cards and course are organized by sales tasks and the sales cycle, not by tools. Nobody studies a tool for its own sake here; you pick a sales task, and the tool comes with it.”

Region and channel adaptation notes

  • Translation/localization: for international field teams, lean into the translation card and the localization angle in first contact and presentations; always have a native speaker sanity-check anything customer-facing.
  • Language of the platform: it is in English today, but nobody has to wait: Chrome translates the whole platform in one click, and the platform’s own language guide (the globe icon on every page) shows how, opening with a recognition line in Kärcher’s 12 languages. Only the spoken audio stays English. Beyond that, the platform is built for full language versions — a language edition is a complete platform with every text and audio track in that language. If your market needs one, say so; languages follow demand.
